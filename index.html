QUESTION 1: WHY DO WE NEED LAPLACE TRANSFORMS?

From Differential Equations in \(t\) to Algebra in \(s\): Understanding the Laplace Transform

Imagine you are given an Initial Value Problem (a differential equation with starting conditions):

\[ x''(t) + 4x(t) = 0, \quad x(0) = 1, \quad x'(0) = 0 \]

Normally, you solve this directly as a differential equation in time (\(\color{#7dd3fc}{t}\)) using calculus techniques and then solving for constants.

Laplace Transforms use a completely different strategy: Instead of solving calculus in the time domain, we apply an integral transform to move into a new world called the \(s\)-domain where derivatives become simple multiplication and initial conditions are baked right in!

Section 6: How Does This Solve Differential Equations?

Why Multiplying by \(e^{-st}\) Turns Derivatives \(f'(t)\) into Algebra \(sF(s) - f(0)\)

The Laplace transform isn't just a random integral—the kernel \(e^{-st}\) was specifically chosen because of what happens when it meets a derivative \(f'(t)\) inside an integral!

1 Why \(e^{-st}\)? (Integration by Parts!)

In a differential equation, we don't know \(f(t)\) yet, so how can we integrate \(f'(t)e^{-st}\)? We use Integration by Parts (\(\int u\,dv = u\cdot v - \int v\,du\)) with \(u = e^{-st}\) and \(dv = f'(t)\,dt\) to shift the derivative off \(f'(t)\) and onto \(e^{-st}\)!

Because \(e^{-st}\) is an exponential function, differentiating it with respect to \(t\) simply brings down a factor of \(-s\)!

The Derivative Property Proof:
\(\mathcal{L}\{f'(t)\} = \displaystyle\int_0^\infty \color{#7dd3fc}{f'(t)}\color{#fcd34d}{e^{-st}}\,dt\)
\(= \color{#fda4af}{\Big[f(t)e^{-st}\Big]_0^\infty} - \displaystyle\int_0^\infty f(t)\left(\color{#fcd34d}{-s\,e^{-st}}\right)dt\)
\(= \color{#fcd34d}{s}\cdot F(s) - f(0)\)
2 Why \(s > a\) Makes \(f(0)\) Pop Out

Look at the boundary term \(\left[f(t)e^{-st}\right]_0^\infty\) in Step 1. Why does the upper limit as \(t \to \infty\) vanish?

Look at Graph B above! Whenever \(s > a\), the kernel \(e^{-st}\) crushes \(f(t)\) down to \(0\) as \(t \to \infty\)! And at the lower limit \(t = 0\), \(e^0 = 1\), which automatically bakes your initial condition \(f(0)\) right into the algebraic equation!

Evaluating the Boundary Term (for \(s > a\)):
Upper (\(t \to \infty\)): \(\quad \lim_{t\to\infty} f(t)e^{-st} = 0\)
Lower (\(t = 0\)): \(\quad f(0)\cdot e^0 = f(0)\)
\(\text{Upper} - \text{Lower} = 0 - f(0) = -f(0)\)
